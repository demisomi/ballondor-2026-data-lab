Who deserves the 2026 Ballon d'Or? The answer depends on how you ask.

I scored 30 nominees on the three official criteria, using match logs from 3 Aug 2025 to 19 Jul 2026. Two different fair-looking methods crown two different winners. This page shows why, and what holds up under both. The last section checks it against the real result.

Three ways to judge a footballer

Every nominee gets a score out of 100, built from his individual stats, his team's results and his disciplinary record. A higher score means a better overall showing on those three questions. The only question is who his individual stats are compared with, and that changes the result. So I judged the players three ways and put them side by side.

1. Position groupsLike separate prizes for each job. Forwards are ranked against forwards, midfielders against midfielders, defenders against defenders. This is fair to defenders, who rarely score. The catch is that a defender can look like a superstar just for being the best defender.
2. One yardstickLike one race for everyone. Every player, forward or defender, is measured on the same five things: goals and assists per 90 minutes, goals without penalties, goals and assists in the big games, defensive actions, and minutes played. Everyone is ranked in one list. The catch is that goals and assists carry most of the weight, so forwards have the advantage.
3. The blendLike two judges who average their marks. Take the score from method 1 and the score from method 2 and average them. It keeps some credit for defenders without letting the lane they play in decide the winner.

No method is the truth. When different methods agree on a player, that is a strong sign. When they disagree, the gap itself is the finding.

The method

Three questions, weighted 50 / 30 / 20

The Ballon d'Or asks for individual performance first, then team performance and titles, then class and fair play. Each criterion becomes a score from 0 to 100. The final score is a weighted sum.

50% Individual
30% Team
20% Class
IndividualGoals, assists, shots on target, defensive actions, expected goals where available, and output in the big games.
TeamTrophies won, Champions League stage, league finish and World Cup stage, adjusted for league strength and minutes played.
Class and fair playMeasured by cards only, scaled so a few cards cannot decide the winner.

Why it happens

Fairness to defenders has a price

Each dot pair shows one player's individual score inside his position group (filled) and on the shared yardstick (hollow). Defenders jump when judged against defenders and sink on the shared scale, because goals and assists dominate a shared scale. Sorted by the size of the gap.

A way through

Blend both views and the table settles

Averaging the position-group score and the shared-yardstick score for the individual criterion keeps the credit for defenders without letting a lenient bracket decide the winner. The bar is the blended final score. The line behind it runs from a player's best to his worst rank across all five views.

Blended scoreLine: best to worst rank across 5 views

How to read this. The top chart ranks players by the blended score, where longer bars are better. The lower chart shows how much a player's rank moves depending on the method. A short line means every method agrees on him. A long line means he is a debate, not a certainty.

All three methods are kept on purpose. Each one answers a different question, and comparing them is the analysis. The blend numbers on this page were calculated from the results table.

Stress test

What if the weights were a little different?

Imagine the judges disagree a little about how much each question matters. I tried 2,000 slightly different weightings and counted who came first each time. With the position-group method the title flips between Mendes and Kane. With the blend Kane wins every time.

How to read this. I changed the weights slightly 2,000 times, for example 55 / 25 / 20 or 45 / 35 / 20, and counted how often each player came first. A bar of 100% means he won every time. Two bars near 60% and 40% mean the title is a toss-up that depends on small choices.

Where I agreed, and where I missed

Agreed: Kane first. On the single shared yardstick, Haaland, Dembélé, Olise, Messi, Kvaratskhelia, Mbappé and Yamal all land in my top 10 as well as the real top 10, 8 of the real 10 in all.

Missed: The position-group view made Nuno Mendes first, and the voters placed him 23rd. It also lifted Neves and Upamecano well above their real ranks. Going the other way, Rodri was 4th with the voters and 28th to 29th in my data, and Yamal and Cubarsí were also placed far higher by voters.

The defender effect was a real flaw in the position-group view. The view that treated every player the same tracked the voters best, and the blend landed between the two. One vote is one data point, and voters weigh reputation, the Barcelona and PSG vote split and a full season of narrative. So I am treating this as evidence, not as the answer key. Changing the weights now to match the result would be fitting the answer.

Limits

What this does not know

  • Class is measured by cards only, so it rewards clean play and says little about quality.
  • Goals conceded are not included, which understates elite defenders on dominant teams.
  • The Saudi and MLS league-strength adjustments are assumed, not measured. Sadio Mané's ranking is low-confidence because Saudi pages carry no expected goals.
  • The blend was chosen after I saw the defender effect. Read it as a robustness check, not a prediction.
  • The real ranking is a vote, not a measurement. It tells us what voters valued, not what a fair formula should say.
  • A few positions (for example Olise, Neves, Bruno Fernandes) are my classification of a player's main role.
